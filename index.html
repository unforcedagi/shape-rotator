How to flip

Six ways to change your mind

None of these is a trick of the image. They are all ways of leaning on a decision your visual system is making anyway.

Pick a dot. Decide it’s in front.

Not “wait until it looks like it is” — decide. Attending to one element and assigning it a depth is the most reliable lever anyone has found.

The one the lab literature actually measures — Pastukhov et al. 2012

Blink.

A hard blink, or a second with your eyes shut, wipes the current interpretation. What reassembles is a coin flip you can lean on.

Look slightly away.

In peripheral vision the motion evidence is far weaker, so the percept is cheap to overturn. Catch the flip out of the corner of your eye, then look back.

Move the camera, not the object.

Imagine you are above it looking down; then underneath, looking up. The rotation direction follows the imagined viewpoint.

Cover half.

Put a hand over the top half, settle on a direction from the bottom half alone, then uncover. Then do it the other way round.

Use the help, then fade it.

Turn the help up until the true direction is obvious, press fade, and hold what you saw for the eight seconds it takes to disappear. This is the whole method.

Notes, references and the honest weaknesses

Why this works

The image genuinely does not know. Take a cloud of dots on the surface of a sphere, turn it about the vertical axis, and draw it with an orthographic camera: no perspective, every dot the same size and the same brightness whatever its depth, nothing hidden behind anything. Now mirror the whole thing in depth, so every point at z moves to −z, and turn it the other way. The two produce pixel-identical images, forever, because the projection throws away the coordinate the two versions differ in. This is not an artist’s trick or a near-miss; it is an exact algebraic degeneracy. What you see is therefore not a reading of the evidence — the evidence is silent — it is a construction your visual system commits to and then defends. Wallach and O’Connell called the general phenomenon the kinetic depth effect in 1953: a flat moving shadow spontaneously acquiring a solid three-dimensional form. The reversals are the interesting part. A system that had merely computed the answer would not keep changing it.

What it costs to keep the image honest. Almost every piece of graphics code you would write by reflex is a depth cue, and any one of them silently ends the illusion. Perspective divide: a cue. Scaling dots with distance: a cue. Fog, shading, occlusion, z-sorting with opaque fills, a drop shadow: all cues. So the renderer here is deliberately impoverished. Projection is orthographic — (x, y, z) → (x, y), no divide. At help = 0 every dot in a cloud is the same size and the same alpha and the cloud goes down as one fill per colour, so there is no per-dot state that could leak depth; a wire is stroked as one path at one width; the silhouette is the nonzero-winding union of every triangle filled flat, which is why it has no interior line at all. Every loop is animated from phase = (t mod period) / period and never from an accumulated delta, so the frame at phase 0 is bit-identical to the frame at phase 1 and a recording of one period loops without a seam. The help dial is the same code path with the terms switched on: scale = 1/(1 − help·k·z) for perspective, radius × (1 + help·z·k) for size, alpha falling off with depth. At help = 0 each of those expressions is exactly its identity, which is the property that makes the claim checkable rather than aspirational.

Where the look comes from. Each object spins about its own axis, tipped fifteen to twenty degrees towards you, so the top of it traces a visible circle. Nothing is filled: each one is a few thousand tiny dots sampled uniformly by area over its surface, at one size, with no occlusion. Where the surface turns edge-on, a whole band of it projects into a couple of pixels and the dots stack, so the rim lights itself; where it faces you they spread out and it goes dim. That is the whole shading model, and it is free, because it is a fact about the projection rather than a thing drawn on top of it. Colour follows two safe rules: it can be attached to the object (the tetrahedron’s pink corner is pink in either reading), or it can be a function of the projected image (the ring arrow’s gold-to-blue gradient is keyed to how high a dot lands on the screen). Colouring by depth would have ended the illusion and is used nowhere. If you would rather have none of it, the palette switch in the app’s details drawer has a mono setting that throws all the colour away, which is the honest control.

One technique that did not fit on a card. Slow it down. Longer periods are more ambiguous and much easier to steer; speed stabilises the percept, which is why the drill gets harder as you shorten the period. The period slider is in the details drawer.

Honest weaknesses

They are not equally good, and the order on this page is the honest order. The ring arrow is first because it is the loop that went round and because its silhouette never changes at all, so there is nothing to read but the head going round. The chair is the same trick applied to an object you have opinions about, and it is noticeably stickier for exactly that reason. The straight arrow is the cleanest case in the set: an arrow of revolution is mirror-symmetric about its own vertical plane, so its depth-mirror is the same arrow, and the two readings are the same picture rather than two theories about a picture. The coil is the one place the mirror argument has to be stated carefully — a helix is chiral, so its depth-mirror is the opposite-handed spring rather than the same object; the picture still cannot tell the two readings apart, it just cannot tell you which spring you are looking at either. Sphere and cylinder are the lab standards and they flip freely for almost everyone. The cube is a Necker cube in motion and flips readily, but the flip is as much a figural reversal as a motion reversal, which is a slightly different phenomenon wearing the same coat. The ring is the crispest of the lot for some people and stubborn for others, because thirty-two dots on one ellipse is very little evidence and the percept can lock. The silhouette depends heavily on the shape: this one is a lathe-turned vessel with a spout, which reverses well when the spout is near the edges of its sweep and rather less well when it is pointing straight at you. The last one is the honest failure. It is named dual-axis after Frank Force’s 2019 Best Illusion of the Year, in which a single loop reads as spinning about a vertical axis and about a horizontal one; what is shipped here is a tennis-ball-seam curve turning about an axis that leans out of the screen, so its two readings are “leaning towards me” and “leaning away” — a real second axis, but not the vertical-versus-horizontal read that makes Force’s version famous. Finally: none of this measures your percept. It records the key you pressed. Every number on the site is a number about your reporting, which lags the flip by a reaction time and can be wrong on purpose, and there is no way around that in a browser.

Where the loops come from

Nothing here is copied, traced or embedded — every loop is drawn from scratch in a few hundred lines of canvas code, and the shapes are chosen to echo the ones that travel. The ring arrow is the refresh-arrow loop that circulates as a “which way is it spinning?” post; the silhouette is the spinning-dancer family, which Nobuyuki Kayahara made a mass phenomenon in 2003; the dual-axis knot is named after Frank Force’s 2019 Best Illusion of the Year; the sphere, cylinder and Necker cube are the laboratory stimuli the papers below use, and Michael Bach’s implementation is the reference everyone checks their version against.
